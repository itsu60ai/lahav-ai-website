# LAHAV AI — Hebrew copy style guide

Written after reading how Israeli companies in this exact field actually
write. Sources studied (homepages and service pages, August 2026):

- automotion.co.il — business automation for SMBs
- automagic.co.il — automation and AI consulting for SMBs
- avior-ai.co.il — AI and automation software house
- optimate.co.il — CRM implementation, automation, AI agents
- smartup.co.il — CRM implementation and customer-journey automation
- infines.co.il — digital agency, useful for process-stage phrasing
- olsi.co.il, aran.org.il, locwise.com — custom system development

This document is the authority for tone. It does not override
`docs/SOURCE_OF_TRUTH.md` on claims, which always wins.

---

## 1. The single biggest rule: use the Hebrew agency voice

Israeli business copy in this field runs on two grammatical people, and
using them is most of what makes copy sound native rather than translated.

- **The reader is `אתם` / `שלכם`.** Plural, warm, never `אתה`, never formal
  third person.
- **What we do together is first person plural future: `נשב`, `נבין`,
  `נאפיין`, `נבנה`, `נחבר`, `נעבור`.** This is the move that turns a
  brochure into a conversation. Every competitor studied does it.

Weak, and what this site used to do everywhere:

> אנחנו בונים מערכות שמחברות את התהליכים בעסק

Native:

> נשב, נבין איך העסק עובד היום, ונחבר את מה שצריך לדבר אחד עם השני

## 2. Be concrete. Abstraction is the main failure mode

Every site studied lists real, recognisable tasks: reminders, follow-up
messages, moving details between systems, appointment confirmations,
payment reminders. Nobody says "processes". They say the thing.

- Bad: `מייעלים תהליכים עסקיים`
- Good: `אתם מפסיקים להעתיק פרטים מוואטסאפ לאקסל`

If a sentence could appear on any technology company's website, it is not
finished.

## 3. Sentence rhythm: fragments, then one explaining sentence

The pattern is a short punchy fragment or two, then one longer sentence
that explains. Fragments are welcome and normal in Hebrew marketing copy.

> העבודה החוזרת רצה ברקע. אתם נשארים עם ההחלטות.
> כל מה שחוזר על עצמו כל שבוע אפשר להגדיר פעם אחת ולתת למערכת לעשות.

Avoid three long sentences in a row. Avoid a paragraph with no fragment
in it.

## 4. Questions as headings, sparingly

`מה זה בעצם אומר?`, `נשמע מוכר?`, `למה זה טוב?` are all standard and they
work. Use at most two per page, or they become a tic.

## 5. Vary the headline construction

The construction `X אחד/אחת. Y:` is effective once and grating three
times. This site had it on Home, on Services and on the service pages.
Rotate between:

- Statement plus counter-statement: `לא חסרים כלים. חסר שהם יעבדו יחד.`
- Negation then promise: `בלי להחליף מערכת. בלי להתחיל מהתחלה.`
- Plain question: `מה מהדברים האלה קורה אצלכם?`
- Direct benefit: `כל לקוח במקום אחד.`

## 6. Calls to action are invitations, not nouns

Competitors use `בואו נדבר`, `לתיאום פגישה`, `אני רוצה לשמוע עוד`,
`השאירו פרטים`. All are verbs or invitations.

`קביעת שיחת Discovery` failed twice: it is a noun phrase, and it drops an
English word into a Hebrew button. Use `לתיאום שיחת היכרות`, and let the
supporting line carry the detail (`30 דקות, בלי התחייבות`).

Keep English out of Hebrew CTAs entirely. `CRM` is fine, it is how Israeli
business owners actually say it. `Discovery` is not.

## 7. Where we deliberately differ from the field

Most sites studied lean on numbers: `שיפור של 40% בפרודוקטיביות`,
`עלייה של 20 עד 50% במכירות`, price ranges, delivery windows.

**We do not use any of those.** No performance figures, no client counts,
no revenue claims, no years of experience, no delivery promises. See
`docs/SOURCE_OF_TRUTH.md`.

So our credibility has to come from somewhere else, and the answer is
**specificity about the process instead of specificity about results.**
Being unusually clear and honest about how the work runs, what we ask, and
where the limits are, is the differentiator. Where competitors write
`תוצאות מוכחות`, we write what actually happens in week one.

Saying plainly what a thing does *not* do also buys trust:

> אוטומציה טובה לא מחליטה במקומכם.

## 8. Banned

- The em dash character. Permanent rule. Use a comma, colon, period, or rewrite.
- Vendor, platform and framework names in public copy.
- Invented metrics, results, client counts, experience claims.
- English words inside Hebrew CTAs.
- `מייעלים תהליכים`, `פתרונות מתקדמים`, `חדשנות`, `לוקחים את העסק לשלב הבא`
  and any other phrase that survives having its subject swapped out.

## 9. The seven-question audit, per page

1. Would a business owner with no technical background understand every sentence?
2. Is there a single sentence that could belong to any other company?
3. Does the page use `אתם` and `נ-` verbs, or does it only describe us?
4. Is there at least one concrete, recognisable everyday example?
5. Is the headline construction different from the previous page's?
6. Does anything promise a result we cannot stand behind?
7. Read aloud: does any sentence sound translated?

## 10. Long-form structure (articles and guides)

Added 2026-09-02, distilled from the shape of a popular Hebrew AI-tips
blog the client pointed to as a reference for "how good Hebrew copy is
built" — the STRUCTURE only, described here in our own words. No text
from that or any other outside source is stored anywhere in this repo;
see F-47 in `docs/SOURCE_OF_TRUTH.md`.

The pattern that makes a long how-to piece easy to start and easy to
act on:

1. **Open on a scene the reader already recognises**, not a definition.
   Name the frustrating moment before naming the topic.
2. **State one underlying principle early**, in a single sentence, and
   say plainly that it is the one thing worth remembering. Everything
   after it should read as a consequence of that one sentence, not a
   new, unrelated tip.
3. **Grade the advice by difficulty**, easiest and most universal first,
   more specific or advanced later. A reader who only wants the basics
   should be able to stop partway through and already have value.
4. **Number concrete steps**, each with its own short bold label
   followed by one to three sentences of plain explanation. No step
   should require reading the ones after it to make sense.
5. **Back a claim with one real, specific number when we have one we
   can stand behind** — this site's own rule against invented figures
   (section 7) still wins; only use a number that is actually true for
   us.
6. **Name and knock down a myth or two near the end**, phrased as
   "people think X, actually Y" — short, myth then correction, no
   elaboration needed.
7. **Close by restating the one principle from step 2** in a single
   summary line, then a low-pressure next step or invitation, not a
   hard sales pitch.
